02Motivation

Choosing a year of your life from six thousand miles away

Every August, several thousand people arrive in New Haven having already signed a lease for an apartment they have never seen, in a country whose rental conventions nobody explained to them, in their second or third language.

I was one of them. The process is not hard because the information is secret. It is hard because the information is scattered, inconsistent, and silent about the things that cost the most. A listing tells you the monthly rent. It does not tell you that the unit next door, advertised for a hundred dollars more, includes heat and will therefore be cheaper over a Connecticut winter. It does not tell you that the standard student lease in this city starts on 1 June while the academic year starts in late August, so the default is to pay for two months of an empty apartment. It does not tell you that the deposit being asked for exceeds what state law permits.

Yale's own guidance is honest about the gap. The Office of International Students and Scholars points incoming students at listing sites and then adds that many New Haven properties are multi-family houses whose landlords advertise with a sign in the window rather than online, and that asking classmates is a normal part of the search. That is good advice and it is also an admission: the system assumes you are already here, already know the city, and already know how American leases work.

What it costs people today

Three costs, in rough order of how much they hurt:

  • The utility surprise. U.S. Census data for New Haven puts the median gross rent at ${{CITY_GROSS}} and the median contract rent at ${{CITY_CONTRACT}}. The difference — ${{CITY_UTIL}} a month — is what the tenant pays for utilities, and it is invisible in a rent figure. Across the {{N_TRACTS}} tracts in this region that burden ranges from ${{UTIL_MIN}} to ${{UTIL_MAX}} a month. At the top of that range it is {{UTIL_PCT_OF_RENT}}% of the median contract rent.
  • The calendar gap. A June lease and an August arrival means two months of rent for an empty apartment. On a typical two-bedroom in this snapshot that is roughly $3,000–$4,400 paid for nothing, and most people discover it after signing.
  • The time. Weeks of evenings spent cross-referencing listing sites, Facebook groups and a WhatsApp group of strangers, with no way to compare two options on the thing that actually matters.

The number that made me build this

In this dataset, the tenant's utility burden varies by up to ${{UTIL_MAX}} a month between census tracts of the same city. That is not a rounding error on a rent comparison. It is larger than the entire difference most people are choosing between when they pick one apartment over another — and no listing site shows it.

03What is new here

The comparison tools answer a different question

I looked first, and there is a lot in this space. None of it is aimed at the person this app is for.

What existsWhat it doesWhat it does not do
Rentometer Takes an address, bedroom count and a proposed rent; returns whether that rent is normal for the area. Compares advertised rent to advertised rent. Utilities, lease calendar and deposit legality are out of scope.
Zillow Rent Zestimate Estimates rent from comparable nearby rentals and the home's features. Same blind spot, plus thin coverage of New Haven's multi-family stock.
Apartment List “Rate My Rent” Answers “is my rent fair?” at neighbourhood level. Neighbourhood medians, not this unit's total cost.
Yale Off-Campus Housing Lists apartments near campus with price, bedroom and amenity filters. A directory. No judgement, no cost modelling, no risk checking. Its own “international friendly” flag is set on 0 of {{N_LISTINGS}} listings.
ChatGPT with the listing pasted in Will discuss the listing fluently. Has no New Haven rent data, no census tract, and no Connecticut rule file. It will produce a confident number it cannot source, which is precisely the failure mode that hurts this user.

Each of these tools assumes four things about its user: that they understand American rental conventions, that they can go and view the apartment, that monthly rent is the thing to compare, and that they know the neighbourhood. For someone deciding from abroad before they arrive, all four are false. That is the gap this app is built in.

What the Rent Desk does differently

  • Total cost, not rent. Twelve months of rent, utilities grounded in census measurement for that specific tract, internet, laundry, insurance, parking, furnishing, and the calendar gap — with every line labelled as measured or assumed.
  • Connecticut law, checked. Deposit caps, escrow, interest and return deadlines, applied by a rule engine against the text of the advertisement.
  • Built for someone who cannot visit. The output ends in a list of questions specific to this listing's unknowns, written to be handed to whoever is doing the video walkthrough.
  • Ranked by total cost, not by advertised rent. The shortlist is ordered by what twelve months actually costs and how far it is from the address the person has to reach, not by the number in the headline.
  • Honest blanks. A field the advertisement does not state is reported as unknown. In this sample the parser admits {{MEAN_UNKNOWN}} unknown fields per listing rather than filling them with plausible guesses.

04The agents

Three model calls, and a firm rule about who may touch a number

The architecture follows one principle: no agent is asked to do arithmetic that matters. Language models are used for the things they are genuinely good at — reading messy prose, explaining a rule to a frightened reader, writing a sharp question. Every dollar figure is computed in ordinary Python from committed public data. That is why the same listing produces the same numbers twice, and why hostile text inside a listing cannot move a price.

Why this model

Everything runs on gpt-6-luna through Portkey, the course model. Three properties of the task made it a good fit rather than a constraint: it returns reliable structured output, so ParsedListing and the rest come back as typed objects rather than prose to be re-parsed; it reads images, verified before the app was designed, which is what makes the “screenshot from a group chat” path possible; and it reports cached token counts, which is what made the cost analysis in section 5 measurable rather than estimated. No agent in this app needs a frontier reasoning model, because the hard reasoning has been moved into Python on purpose.

The defence is structural

The intake agent — the only component that reads attacker-controlled text — is constructed with no tools at all. It cannot call anything. Its output type is a fixed schema, so the worst a perfect injection can do is corrupt field values, which the deterministic layer then sanity-checks against public data. This is not a filter that can be argued with; it is an absence of capability.

One agent has a tool, and which one is the argument

The advisor can call find_comparable_listings, which reads the committed snapshot and returns what else that money buys in the same census tract. Intake cannot call anything.

That asymmetry is the design, not an inconsistency. By the time the advisor runs, the attacker-controlled text is gone: it sees only values the deterministic layer produced. Its tool reads committed public data and does nothing else - no writes, no network, no reach back into the listing. Handing the same tool to intake would give a hostile advertisement something to call, which is exactly the capability the defence is built on removing.

Before any of that: ranking 147 listings for nothing

Most people do not arrive with one advertisement in hand; they arrive with a budget, a household, and a building they have to reach every morning. So the first screen ranks the whole snapshot against those three things — and does it with zero model calls.

That is not a saving trick, it is the same principle as the rest of the app. Which apartments are worth opening is arithmetic: the census utility measurement for each listing's own tract, the twelve-month simulation, the fair-price comparison, and the distance from each listing's coordinates to wherever the person typed. Running three agents over 147 listings would cost about ${{RANK_VS_AGENTS_USD}} and several minutes to answer a question that takes 19 milliseconds and nothing. The model is spent on the one listing they then choose to open.

The ranking also inherits the rule file's judgement rather than contradicting it. A listing more than 35% under its local baseline is not promoted as a bargain — that is the same threshold SCAM-PRICE-TOO-GOOD uses, and in this snapshot it catches eight listings that are per-room prices quoted as whole-unit rent. They are demoted and labelled, not hidden.

Telling someone what they are looking at

Most of what this app knows is useless to a person who has never rented in the United States, because they do not yet have the words. An introduction opens on the first visit with four tabs: how the three steps work, what actually costs money, a glossary of the vocabulary - gross rent against contract rent, escrow, guarantor, triple decker, walk-up - and a tab written specifically for someone signing from abroad: what a landlord will ask them for, what to do with no American credit history, and the three conditions that must all be true before any money moves. It can be dismissed for the day or reopened from the header, and its text lives in data/guide.json so it can be corrected without a deploy.

Where the shortlist actually is

Rent is a geographic question and a ranked list hides that. The shortlist is therefore drawn on an OpenStreetMap view of New Haven: numbered pins matching the list, a marker on the place the person typed, and a popup on each pin with the real monthly cost. Clicking a pin selects that listing, so the list and the map are two views of one answer, and a full-screen view docks the list beside the map for comparing them.

Getting there also has to accept what people actually type. An earlier version asked only the Census geocoder, which resolves numbered street addresses and nothing else: against sixteen real Yale queries it found four. The app now carries a committed gazetteer of {{N_PLACES}} named places — {{N_PLACES_CURATED}} of them Yale's own schools, residential colleges, libraries and landmarks, resolved and bounds-checked at build time, the rest harvested from OpenStreetMap — matched without a network call and forgivingly enough that med school, divinity school and peabody all land. Anything still unmatched is geocoded live. Neither geocoder needs an API key, so the app's requirement stays at one.

Serving every incoming international student at Yale — roughly three thousand a year, twenty listings each — costs about ${{COST_YALE}} a year in tokens. The cost is not the obstacle to deploying this; it is a rounding error against a single month's rent for one student.

What was done to cut cost

  1. Move the reasoning out of the model. Fit scoring, cost simulation, fair price and the entire rule engine are Python. An earlier design had five agents; three of them were replaced by functions that are faster, free, and deterministic.
  2. Put the stable material first. Each agent's prompt opens with reference blocks — the New Haven listing glossary, the Connecticut rule file, the census baseline — that are byte-identical on every run, so the provider can cache them. {{CACHE_RATE}}% of all input tokens are now served from cache at a tenth of the fresh rate.
  3. Keep the model out of the shortlist. Ranking 147 listings is arithmetic, so it runs with no model call at all: $0 and 19 milliseconds against roughly ${{RANK_VS_AGENTS_USD}} and several minutes if the three agents were run over the whole snapshot. Nearly all the browsing in this app is therefore free, and the paid work happens once the person has chosen.
  4. Cap the budget. Every run carries hard request and token ceilings, so a loop or a hostile input cannot run up a bill.

06Safety

The listing is written by whoever wants your money

This app has an unusual threat model for a student project: its single untrusted input is authored by the party with a financial interest in the outcome. A landlord who wants a unit rented has motive to manipulate an automated reviewer, and listing text goes straight into a prompt.

Three layers, only one of which actually holds

  1. Sanitise. Zero-width characters, bidirectional controls and CSS-hidden passages are stripped before anything reads the text, and what was removed is kept and shown to the user.
  2. Detect. Eleven injection phrasings are matched deterministically. No model call, so an attacker cannot talk the detector round.
  3. Isolate. The agent that reads the text has no tools and can only emit a fixed schema, and every figure is computed afterwards in Python from public data. This is the layer that holds. Layers 1 and 2 exist to make the attempt visible; layer 3 makes it pointless.

Agent malfunction

The failure mode that would hurt this user most is not an attack; it is a confident invented number. Four things guard against it:

  • Unknown is a value. The parser names every field it could not fill — {{MEAN_UNKNOWN}} per listing in this sample — instead of guessing. Those gaps become the questions in the final section rather than silent fabrications.
  • No invented law. The risk agent may only cite rule ids from a reviewed file. Anything it notices on its own must be prefixed OBSERVED- and marked informational, so a reader can tell a statute from a hunch at a glance.
  • Findings cannot be dropped. After the risk agent runs, the orchestrator compares its output against the deterministic findings and re-inserts anything missing. A suppression attack cannot succeed even if the model complies.
  • Measured and assumed are visually distinct in the product, not just in this report.

Overspending

Every run carries hard ceilings: {{MAX_REQ}} model requests, {{MAX_IN}} input and {{MAX_OUT}} output tokens, and listing text is capped at {{MAX_CHARS}} characters before it reaches a prompt. A runaway loop or a megabyte of pasted hostile text terminates rather than billing.

08Limits and next steps

What does not work yet

  • One source. The snapshot is {{N_LISTINGS}} listings from Yale's marketplace. It can now be refreshed on a schedule — scripts/refresh_listings.py diffs against what is held, records price moves, and marks vanished listings inactive rather than deleting them, so how long a place has sat unrented becomes answerable. But landlords who advertise with a sign in the window — a large part of this market, by Yale's own description — remain invisible to it.
  • Census medians are a bearing, not a valuation. ACS covers all rentals in a tract, not student housing, and does not adjust for condition, floor or renovation. The app says so on screen next to the number.
  • Utility components are assumed. Only the utility total is measured. How it splits across heat, electricity and the rest is an assumption, labelled everywhere it is used.
  • No lease review. The rule file covers deposits and scam patterns. Most of what goes wrong in a tenancy is in the lease, which this app never sees.
  • Commute is the distance the source reports. No routing, no winter, no night-time safety data.
  • English only. For an app aimed at international students this is the most embarrassing gap on the list.

With another month

  1. The accuracy test that matters. Recruit ten students who moved to New Haven this year, run their actual listing through the desk, and compare the twelve-month prediction against what they really paid. That number — prediction error against reality — is the one that would make this credible to a housing office.
  2. Lease upload. The deposit rules already exist; pointing them at the lease rather than the advertisement is where the real legal risk lives.
  3. Translation. The advice and the questions in Korean, Mandarin, Hindi and Spanish. The model can already do it; the UI needs to ask.
  4. Side-by-side comparison. Users do not evaluate one apartment, they choose between three. Total-cost comparison across saved candidates is the obvious next screen.
  5. Hand it to OISS. The strongest version of this is not a personal tool but something Yale's international student office links from its housing page.
